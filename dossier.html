Career Record (Readable Document)

This is different from the backup above — it's not for restoring into the app, it's a plain-language document of your profile, experience, education, and case files, meant to be pasted into Google Docs or Word for your own records.

These are reflection prompts, not a test — there's no scoring, and no pattern here is a fact about you until you recognize it as true. The core idea: the things you consistently want, expect, or ask of others often point at something you value, want more of, or haven't fully given yourself permission to pursue. That's a real, useful lens (it borrows from projection and individuation concepts in depth psychology) — but it's one lens among several below. Trust what actually resonates and discard what doesn't. Write freely; only you ever see this.
The shape of this page: start with what's true in you, then look at the outer world, then use the friction between the two as a clue — not evidence something's wrong with you, just information. What you build in Master Profile and Case Files is that alignment finding its way out into the world.
Showing just this step.
1. The Mirror Exercise

Think of something you often catch yourself wanting, expecting, or telling others — a coworker, a partner, a friend, even a stranger. "I wish they'd just be on time." "Why can't people work harder." "I want them to be honest with me." Write it down, then sit with what it might be pointing back at in you.

2. Energy & Flow Log

Over the next while, jot down moments — at work, in a project, anywhere — where time disappeared and you felt fully absorbed, versus moments that quietly drained you even if nothing was "wrong." Patterns here are often more honest than any list of interests you'd write from memory.

3. Admiration & Envy

Who do you find yourself admiring, or — more tellingly — quietly envying? Envy is worth taking seriously as data: it often marks something you want for yourself but haven't let yourself claim, rather than something to feel guilty about.

4. The Terrain: How Careers Tend to Grow
The reflection above is about what's true of you. This is about the map you're walking on. Two different questions worth keeping separate: "how do people typically progress once they're on a track" (fairly stable, shown below) versus "which tracks are worth building toward" (genuinely uncertain, and changes faster than any static page can responsibly claim). For the second question, treat what's below as a way of thinking about it, not a forecast — and use the "check current thinking" links to verify against the actual, current internet rather than trusting a snapshot that can't update itself.
Fading, Evergreen, and Emerging — as Patterns, Not Predictions

Naming specific job titles as "dying" or "the future" is exactly the kind of claim that ages badly sitting in a downloaded file with no way to update itself. What holds up better is the shape of the pattern — recognize it in whatever role or industry you're actually looking at.

5. Drawing the Threads Together

After a few entries above, reread them and look for repetition — not one grand answer, but a small set of themes that keep showing up. What do they suggest about the kind of work, environment, or role that would actually fit you?

That last button just pre-fills the "known for" field in your Master Profile's Summary Assistant with these themes — nothing is overwritten without you reviewing it there.

6. Turning This Into a Goal

This is your own pace, not anyone else's ladder — a direction to move in, not a scoreboard to compare against. It grows out of what you noticed above, not the other way around: start from the friction, not from a title you think you're supposed to want. Dates are kept vague on purpose. And this works best when it turns into something you actually do — a case file, a conversation, a small step — rather than staying something you keep refining on the page.

Everything above is a fill-in-the-blank template — useful, but it can't generate new phrasing or catch a weak bullet the way an actual conversation with an AI assistant can. These prompts are built to hand your own facts to a tool like Claude and get a real draft back. Copy one, paste your details into the brackets, and paste the whole thing into an AI chat. Each is written to discourage the AI from inventing details you didn't give it — that's the single most common way AI-drafted resume content goes wrong, so watch for it anyway and check every claim before it goes in a real application.
